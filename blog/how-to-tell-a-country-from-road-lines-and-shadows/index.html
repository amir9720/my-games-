How Can You Tell What Country a Photo Was Taken In From Road Lines and Sun Shadows?

ZP By ZinPlay Editorial Team · Last updated Nov 25, 2026
Field Guide
Share: 𝕏

You have probably seen those jaw-dropping clips where an online geography detective looks at a blurry picture of an empty dirt road—no Eiffel Tower, no skyline, not even a legible street sign—and somehow says, "Yeah, that's rural Norway, maybe thirty miles north of Oslo." It looks like a magic trick at first, or some secret database nobody else has access to.

In reality, open-source geolocation (often shortened to Geo-OSINT) is built on boring everyday infrastructure that locals stop noticing after living around it for years. Every government paints its highways a slightly different way, buys reflective roadside posts from different local factories, and sits under a specific angle of sunlight. Once you know what to look for, a plain strip of asphalt starts reading like an ID card.

Why Does the Color of Road Paint Give Away the Region?

The fastest way to cut the world map in half is to stop looking at the scenery and look straight down at the paint beneath the tires. Different continents settled on completely different color conventions decades ago:

  • Double yellow centerlines with white outer edges: This is the classic signature of the Americas—the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, and much of South America. Outside the Americas, South Korea and Taiwan also use prominent yellow centerlines, which immediately separates them from neighboring Japan (which favors crisp white dashed lines on most roads).
  • All-white road markings: Almost all of mainland Europe, the United Kingdom, and Australia rely on white paint for both the center dashes and the outer borders.
  • The famous Scandinavian giveaway: If you are staring at dark evergreen forests, snow poles, and European-style guardrails—yet the center dashed line is bright yellow—you are almost certainly in Norway. Cross the border into neighboring Sweden or Finland, and that exact same highway switches back to white paint.
  • Yellow outer shoulder lines with a white center: Flip the usual pattern upside down—solid yellow lines along the outside edges and white dashes down the middle—and you have one of the cleanest fingerprints for South Africa and neighboring Botswana.

How Can a Simple Shadow Tell You Which Hemisphere You Are In?

After the paint, look up at the sky or down at the ground shadows next to a lamppost. Because the Earth is tilted and orbits the sun along the equator, the midday sun never passes directly overhead once you travel north of the Tropic of Cancer or south of the Tropic of Capricorn.

That gives investigators one of the most dependable compass tricks in the field:

  • Sun sitting in the southern sky (shadows pointing roughly north): You are in the Northern Hemisphere—Europe, North America, or northern Asia.
  • Sun sitting in the northern sky (shadows pointing roughly south): You are in the Southern Hemisphere—Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Argentina, Chile, or most of Brazil.

That single check saves you from one of the most common geolocation blunders: mixing up rural Australia with California or rural Spain. The dry hills, eucalyptus trees, and sun-bleached grass can look nearly identical in a quick glance, yet checking where the sun sits—and which side of the road cars drive on—separates them in three seconds flat.

What Tiny Clues Sit Right Next to the Shoulder?

Once road paint and sun angle narrow your guess down to a single continent, the small hardware along the curb does the fine-tuning. Geolocation researchers pay close attention to roadside bollards (those short plastic or concrete posts that reflect headlights at night):

  • White posts with a slanted black top cap and small red rectangle: Classic central Europe—especially Germany, Austria, and Poland (Poland adds a bold red wrap-around band near the top).
  • Tall skinny orange sticks with a white reflector band: Snow-plow depth markers used across Norway, Sweden, and Iceland so winter drivers can still tell where the ditch begins under three feet of snow.
  • Simple white flat posts with a red strip on one side and white on the other: The signature roadside marker across thousands of miles of Australian outback highways.
  • License plate proportions (even when blurred): You do not need to read a single letter on a parked car. A wide, bright yellow rear plate points immediately to the United Kingdom or the Netherlands; twin blue strips on both the left and right ends point to France or Italy; and a solid blue bar across the top edge flags a Mercosur plate from Brazil, Argentina, or Uruguay.

A 5-Step Checklist for Reading Any Street Photo Like a Detective

The biggest mistake beginners make is jumping straight to a gut hunch ("looks kind of like Italy") and ignoring clues that contradict it. Run through this quick five-step order instead:

  1. Check the driving side first: Which way are parked cars facing, and which side do road signs face? Left-hand driving immediately cuts the list down to places like the UK, Japan, Australia, South Africa, India, and Thailand.
  2. Check the hemisphere using the sun or shadows: If the midday sun is clearly north, cross off Europe and North America right away.
  3. Read the asphalt paint: Match yellow centerlines, all-white European dashes, or yellow outer shoulders to narrow down the region.
  4. Scan the curb hardware: Spot bollards, utility pole materials (wooden vs. concrete ladder-style poles), and license plate shapes or color bars.
  5. Confirm with climate and tiny letter accents: Look at red iron-rich soil, white birch trunks, or unique alphabet characters like German ß, Norwegian Ø, Polish Ł, or Turkish Ğ—and if you enjoy digital clue-hunting too, try pairing street sleuthing with desktop forensics in OS Sleuth.

FAQ: Does the sun shadow hemisphere trick work everywhere on Earth?

Not quite everywhere. It is rock-solid across mid- and high-latitude countries like Canada, France, Japan, Australia, and Argentina. Between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn—places like Thailand, southern India, Colombia, or northern Brazil—the midday sun shifts slightly north or south depending on the season, so you should rely more on road lines, scripts, and driving side there.

FAQ: Why do countries like Japan, the UK, and Australia still drive on the left?

Most of it traces back to pre-car road habits and imperial history. In medieval Europe and feudal Japan, travelers kept left so a right-handed rider's sword arm faced oncoming strangers. Britain codified left-hand travel and exported it across Commonwealth nations like Australia, India, and South Africa, while continental Europe and the United States standardized around right-hand wagon lanes.
